Abstract

What is SPRINT?

A practical IoT solution — not a theoretical exercise

We have developed a Smart Spraying System to help automate the water, pesticide and liquid fertilizer distribution in farms or even small gardens. Our main goal was to cut down on manual labour by using an ESP-01 Wi-Fi microcontroller for remote operation.

The hardware configuration is quite simple: ESP-01, relay, up and down voltage regulators, a 12V step-up/step-down pressure pump and solenoid valves, all controlled by a central liquid source. We created a mobile app that connects via Wi-Fi, enabling the user to activate the spray, select the spray liquid and monitor the system in real-time.

Since the system is cloud-based (Firebase), it does not require any fixed schedules and can be adjusted to fit the field's needs. The switching logic is controlled by the relay, and the voltage circuits ensure the stability of power supply to the various components. We created a modular design so we can add soil moisture sensors and weather APIs later and prevent it from spraying during rain.

Basically, this is good for any size garden — from a terrace garden to an open field. It is an affordable and easily scalable solution to enter the precision farming era. The total component cost remained less than Rs. 1,500, and Firebase Spark Plan is free.

Chapter 1

Problem Statement & Motivation

Why does this project exist?

1.1 Introduction

The agriculture sector is still the main pillar of the Indian economy today. It accounts for a colossal share of GDP and the lives of the majority of our population. But on the ground, the methods are still limited to the past — small farms rely nearly exclusively on manual labour for basic operations such as chemical spraying and irrigation.

Manual spraying has the greatest disadvantage in terms of inefficiency. It is virtually impossible to ensure even application when a chemical has to be applied by a walk-behind heavy tank in poor fields. At times it happens that plants are completely covered with chemicals while the surrounding plants remain dry. Long-term effects of this erratic coverage include reduced yields and poor soil quality. The chemicals are also destroyed by evaporation as spraying is often carried out during peak daylight hours. Overnight downpours may simply get rid of the high-cost treatments altogether.

The same issue exists in urban areas with rooftop farming and terrace gardens. Smaller-scale systems are in dire need of automated and compact tools since conventional commercial farming equipment is too cumbersome for a home environment. Our project addresses this very technological gap.

1.2 Motivation

This project began with a rather mundane observation: on most of the small farms and in most of the terrace gardens, the spraying is either manual or controlled by a simple timer. Both are unhealthy for different reasons. Manual spraying is tiring, imprecise and exposes the applicator to pesticide for long periods of time. Timer-based systems are a bit better, but still very dumb — they will spray whether it rained an hour ago or the tank is empty.

We spoke to a few people directly involved in this. One operates a rooftop facility in West Delhi using a mechanical timer for drip. His principal objection was that he did not know whether it had fired while he was at work. Another small farmer from Haryana cited irregular spraying — some rows getting soaked and others barely covered. They didn't have any problem with technology; it was just that they couldn't find anything simple and cheap enough.

The ESP-01 was cheap enough (less than Rs. 150) and Firebase is a solution that doesn't require creating our own server. The Android app connected them. The intention was not to create anything complicated — just something that's actually used.

1.3 Problem Statement

The prototype must satisfy the following requirements:

  • Input: Firebase Realtime Database must receive commands from a user-operated Android app.
  • Controller: The ESP-01 must fetch these commands in near real-time and act as the primary controller for activating output devices.
  • Output: Based on values read from Firebase, the relay module must switch the solenoid valve and pressure pump on/off, allowing controlled spraying of fluids.
  • Power-efficient, easy to install, and compatible with mobile networks via local Wi-Fi.
  • Equally suitable for rural and urban agricultural setups.

Chapter 2

Literature Survey

What already exists, where it falls short, and what SPRINT does differently

2.1 Related Work & Technology

Many research studies and actual projects have illustrated the benefits of implementing smart irrigation using microcontrollers. Consistent themes in the literature include:

  • Use of NodeMCU and ESP8266 modules for controlling actuators via smartphone apps
  • Firebase Realtime Database usage for real-time monitoring and remote control
  • Solenoid valves and relay modules for automating the process of fluid delivery
  • Mobile graphical interfaces created with MIT App Inventor, Android Studio, or custom solutions

Our most helpful source was an instructive guide by RandomNerdTutorials on IoT communication control of ESP8266 GPIOs via Firebase. It provided the basic foundation for cloud communication, saving significant time with library integration. Almost all other resources used the bulkier NodeMCU boards or costlier platforms — justifying the ESP-01 selection for this project.

The Gap SPRINT Fills

While existing solutions offer partial automation, there is a clear need for a low-cost, compact, and modular spraying solution specifically designed for small-scale farms and urban farming scenarios like terrace gardens. Traditional timer-based systems are rigid and lack adaptability to dynamic agricultural needs, often leading to resource wastage.

2.3 Libraries & Tools Used

A cloud-controlled smart spraying system was successfully implemented on the resource-limited ESP-01 by carefully selecting software libraries. Each tool had to be the best fit — flash memory and RAM on this equipment are extremely low.

01
Firebase ESP Client (Mobizt)

Backbone of all communication between ESP8266 and Firebase RTDB. Full CRUD operations, secure token-based auth, real-time data sync, inbuilt reconnection and error handling.

02
WiFiManager (by tzapu)

Opens a captive portal at first boot. User sets network SSID and password via a web browser. Credentials stored in EEPROM for future use — no re-flashing ever needed.

03
ESP8266WiFi

Core Wi-Fi library. Handles SSID scanning, IP address management, signal monitoring and event handling for disconnections.

04
ArduinoJson

All Firebase entries are stored as JSON. ArduinoJson provides efficient, memory-friendly encoding and decoding on the tight ESP-01 heap (96KB RAM).

05
ESP8266WebServer

Creates a small HTTP server on ESP-01. Used for a local LAN status page — offline diagnostics without requiring Firebase connectivity.

06
DNSServer + ESP_EEPROM

DNSServer hijacks DNS requests to open the captive portal automatically. EEPROM stores Wi-Fi credentials and user-defined spraying preferences across reboots.

07
NTPClient + WiFiUDP

Gets current time from an NTP server — used for time-stamped logging and scheduling timed spraying tasks.

Chapter 4 — Working Principle

How SPRINT Works

Six steps from user intent to precision spraying — entirely over the cloud

01
📱

User Command Initiation

A command issued by the user from within the mobile application — pressing a button such as "Start Spraying" — sends a key-value pair entry into the Firebase Realtime Database via JSON (spray_status: "on"). Selection of spray mode includes options like "Start Spray" or "Stop Spray" along with real-time monitoring of operation status.

02
☁️

Firebase Modification

This command triggers modification of a certain key entry in the Firebase database in real-time. The user makes a request through the application, Firebase updates the particular field, and it is read by the ESP-01 module in near real-time. The cloud layer also provides opportunities for collecting historical data and conducting analytics.

03
📡

ESP-01 Data Polling

The ESP-01 device always listens to any changes made to the database via the Firebase ESP Client library callback mechanism — therefore, there is no need for periodic polling. This results in near real-time speed. Wi-Fi connection time from initialisation using WiFiManager: four to six seconds. Data sync time: under one second.

04
🧠

Logical Decision Making

The microcontroller evaluates the value of spray_status and determines whether the GPIO-connected pin should be set to HIGH or LOW. All code has been programmed in the Arduino IDE using non-blocking code for real-time operation. Connection issues are covered by a safety mechanism of automatic network reconnection.

05
⚙️

Relay Activation

The relay activates, energising both the 12V DC pressure pump and the 24V DC solenoid valve simultaneously. The relay module maintains isolation between the microcontroller circuitry and the current-drawing hardware components — the sensitive ESP-01 electronics are protected. As a result, liquid is taken inside and sprayed out from the nozzle in one action.

06
💧

Spraying Process & Shutdown

The system sprays until the user issues a "Stop" command through the app or a built-in timeout fires (e.g., 10 seconds — automatic system shut-down). GPIO pin is set to LOW, the relay goes off, spraying stops. The entire procedure allows total remote control, making usage highly efficient and reducing wastage significantly.

Chapter 3 — Hardware

Hardware Requirements

Carefully selected for reliability, cost-effectiveness, and agricultural suitability — all from Lajpat Rai Market

ESP-01 (ESP8266)

Core Control Unit · WiFi MCU

The entire task of central processing is done through this microcontroller. Through this inexpensive yet extremely small module, wireless communication capabilities can be achieved in conjunction with cloud-based Firebase. It requires a steady 3.3V supply and supports two GPIOs — GPIO0 and GPIO2 — used with the relay module and LEDs. Instantaneous actuation takes place as it constantly listens to Firebase for any change in states. Cost: Rs. 80–100 in Lajpat Rai.

3.3V · GPIO0 + GPIO2 · IEEE 802.11 b/g/n · 96KB RAM · 512KB Flash

Relay Module

5V Single-Channel · Power Switch

Handles the difficult task of interfacing the ESP-01 with high-voltage circuits such as 12V DC solenoid valves and pumps. The relay module maintains isolation between the microcontroller circuitry and the current-drawing hardware — protecting the sensitive ESP-01. Dry contact operation tests showed millisecond response. Load operation demonstrated reliable actuation of solenoids and pumps continuously.

5V coil · Single-channel · ESP-01 compatible · 10A rated contacts

Solenoid Valve (24V DC)

Liquid Flow Control · Normally Closed

Controls the movement of liquids — water, insecticide, and fertilizer — stored in separate external tanks. Activated or deactivated according to signals received from Firebase via the relay. Tested three variants: 12V DC (poor flow rate at high pressure), 24V DC (selected — quick, tight seal), and 220V AC (slow, noisy, required additional insulation). The 24V version proved most effective.

24V DC · Normally closed · 1/2″ inlet · Powered via MT3608 booster

Pressure Pump (12V DC)

Water Pressurisation · Mini Diaphragm

Creates the force necessary to push fluid through a nozzle directed at the plant or crop. Can be turned on or off using the relay module. Tested multiple 12V pumps and selected based on: a constant flow of water ideal for spraying, ability to withstand dry operation periods, and performance when working with load generated by the relay. There is one unified inlet pipe — the operator simply fills an external tank with the required fluid.

12V DC · Mini diaphragm · Relay-controlled · Direct 12V power line

LM2596 Buck Converter

Step-Down · 12V → 3.3V for ESP-01

Lowers the power rail voltage from 12V to the 3.3V required by the ESP-01 and relay module. The output voltage remains constant and protected from over-voltage conditions. Critical for preventing the microcontroller from voltage spikes — some components failed during early testing before this was established. Tried 5V/1A adapter (couldn't run both ESP and relay simultaneously) and 5V/2A (not adequate for solenoid and pump simultaneously) before settling on the 12V + converter approach.

Input: up to 40V · Output: 3.3V–35V adjustable · Efficient step-down

MT3608 Boost Converter

Step-Up · 12V → 24V for Solenoid

Boosts the power rail voltage from 12V to the 24V DC needed to actuate the solenoid valve. Separate power lines are maintained for the pump and ESP to prevent interference and ensure consistent power delivery. The modular approach proved beneficial here — switching from a 12V to a 24V solenoid valve was relatively easy thanks to the boost converter in place.

Input: 2–24V · Output: up to 28V · Isolated power path for solenoid

Final Power Configuration (Chapter 5.2.3)

No battery used — voltage fluctuations from Li-ion batteries can damage the module. AC adapter is safer.

Chapter 3 — Software

Software Requirements

Open-source tools chosen for rapid prototyping, zero licensing cost, and proven Firebase stability

Development Tools
Arduino IDE Used for writing all firmware for the ESP-01. Supports ESP8266 boards via the Board Manager. Enables inclusion of Firebase and WiFiManager libraries. Includes Serial Monitor for real-time debug output. Code is written in non-blocking style for real-time operation.
Android Studio Develops the mobile application that interfaces with the Firebase server. Provides users (farmers or system administrators) remote control over the spraying system along with operational status updates. Uses ConstraintLayout UI for a responsive interface.
Cloud Backend
Firebase Realtime Database (RTDB) Serverless backend. All sensor data and command values are stored using a JSON tree structure that supports hierarchical control logic. Completely free under the Firebase Spark Plan — no server costs.
Firebase Authentication Utilized for access management. Firebase Security Rules are defined for limiting users' read/write permission to specific nodes. Token-based authentication ensures secure data transmission.
Firebase ESP Client (Mobizt) Establishes two-way asynchronous communication between ESP-01 and Firebase RTDB. Provides security features and ensures reliable JSON data synchronization. Inbuilt reconnection — critical for unattended agricultural deployments.
Debugging Tools
Serial Monitor (Arduino IDE) Monitors logs generated by the ESP-01 in real-time during development and testing sessions.
ESP Exception Decoder The ESP-01 tends to crash at times and provides undecipherable stack traces. This tool converts them to meaningful function calls and corresponding files — invaluable for debugging firmware crashes.
Android Studio Logcat Shows all logs, exceptions, and system information during Android app execution. Connection errors with Firebase and UI lag become obvious using this tool.
ESP8266WebServer (LAN status page) A small HTTP server on ESP-01 creates a local status page accessible over LAN — used for offline testing and diagnostics when Firebase is temporarily unavailable.
Open-Source Benefits
Cost Efficiency Free major libraries and development tools. No proprietary software or hardware-specific SDKs required. Entire system built, tested, and deployed within a student budget of under Rs. 1,500.
Community Support A large group of global developers maintain the project's libraries. Multiple GitHub helpers means no unsolvable errors.
Rapid Prototyping Platforms like Arduino IDE make prototyping extremely easy and accessible — simply connect the module to a laptop and it is instantly recognized.
Scalability This firmware, coded for the ESP-01, can be adapted for more capable boards such as ESP-32 with virtually no modification — future-proofing the investment.
Chapter 5

Experimentation & Results

The design process was quite challenging — numerous tests had to be carried out to find appropriate components that would work effectively, reliably, and within budget

5.1 Microcontroller Assessment — Why ESP-01?

The choice of microcontroller was possibly the most important decision in the construction process. Many boards underwent testing before one emerged victorious.

❌ Rejected
ESP-32 Unreliable flashing; power wastage through unused Bluetooth; increased consumption. First candidate tested.
❌ Rejected
ESP32-CAM Camera consumed considerable memory; Firebase actions took time; costly and complex — no vision application needed.
❌ Rejected
Raspberry Pi 3B+ Slow boot time; SD card damage risk; more energy consumed; excessive for this project.
❌ Rejected
NodeMCU Relatively bigger in size; more power-consuming; good performance but not better than ESP-01 with Firebase.
❌ Rejected
H5 Stack Expensive for a basic spraying device; the screen and accessories were unnecessary; consumed a lot of power.
✅ Selected
ESP-01 (ESP8266) Compact, cost-efficient, stable Firebase performance with lightweight libraries, quick Wi-Fi recovery after failures.

5.2 Power Supply Testing

Various AC to DC adapters were tried. Multiple combinations were evaluated because the relay, ESP-01, solenoid valve, and pressure pump all operate at different voltage levels.

❌ Failed 5V / 1A Adapter

Could not support running both ESP and relays simultaneously. Relays would not switch on with load.

❌ Insufficient 5V / 2A or 3A Adapter

Better than previous but not adequate for running solenoid and pump simultaneously.

⚠️ Partial 12V DC Adapter (standalone)

Worked reasonably well for relays and small loads, but required converters for ESP and solenoid voltages.

⚠️ Risky 24V DC Adapter

Sufficient for heavy-duty solenoids, but risky for use directly with ESP-01 (rated 3.3V).

✅ Final Choice 12V 2A + LM2596 + MT3608

12V 2A main supply → LM2596 (→3.3V for ESP) + MT3608 (→24V for solenoid) + direct 12V for pump. Worked perfectly.

⚠️ Battery power was not implemented — voltage fluctuation problems from Li-ion batteries could damage the ESP module.

Chapter 6

Conclusion

In general, we were happy with the results of our efforts

As it was mentioned at the very beginning, the main task was to construct a practical, rather than a theoretical solution. And we believe that it was fulfilled. The choice of the board proved correct. We tried several other models prior to using ESP-01 but as was shown by the testing of Chapter 5, the choice was justified.

Used in combination with Firebase and an Android app, the system operated successfully in all test cycles. Speaking about the problems, there was some difficulty concerning the selection of optimal parameters for powering the sensors and the board. Some components failed before we could establish a correct set of parameters, however, the final one using a 12V 2A source along with LM2596 and MT3608 did its job well.

The system was successfully tested even from outside of college premises, demonstrating true remote control. The modular approach proved beneficial — switching from a 12V solenoid valve to the 24V model was relatively easy thanks to the architecture.

The system demonstrated above proves what can be achieved by utilizing the concepts of embedded systems, IoT, and cloud computing on the basis of a practical application. This is currently a reliable platform which will only get better once some additional fine tuning and inclusion of environmental monitoring devices take place, making this system a complete precision agriculture technology solution.

✅ Pros

  • Cheap parts cost — ESP-01 & relay module together cost less than Rs. 200
  • System can be controlled from anywhere on the internet
  • Successfully tested from outside college premises
  • All parts readily available in Lajpat Rai Market
  • Minimal idle power consumption when not spraying
  • Firebase Spark Plan was free — zero cloud costs
  • Easy maintenance due to modular open-source architecture

⚠️ Cons

  • ESP-01 comes with just two GPIO ports that can be used
  • Incorporating additional sensors was challenging due to insufficient GPIO count
  • Entire operation relies entirely on WiFi connectivity
  • No alternative plan in place in case internet connection fails
  • Mobile application works but its user interface is very simple
  • UI enhancement was not taken into account during development
6.2 Future Scope

Future Enhancements

The system functions as intended but can be expanded — here are the planned directions

01
🌡️

Sensor Integration

Adding soil moisture, temperature, and humidity sensors for automatic, condition-based spraying. Currently, one major drawback of the system is the lack of awareness of the environmental situation around it. An incorporation of even a simple soil moisture sensor would make it possible for the ESP-01 to autonomously determine whether spraying is necessary. Since we're limited by the number of GPIO pins, we might want to switch to an ESP-32 for such functionality, which also provides Bluetooth control.

02
🌦️

Weather API Integration

Connecting to OpenWeatherMap API to access real-time weather forecasts and prevent spraying during rainfall or adverse conditions. During testing, the system once sprayed right before rain began — that is a complete waste of water and chemicals. An API from OpenWeatherMap could solve this issue and ensure resource efficiency.

03
☀️

Solar Power Integration

Incorporating solar panels for off-grid operation, making the system ideal for remote fields lacking reliable power infrastructure. The amount of idle power used by ESP-01 is very small — a solar panel and battery pack can effectively power it, making it usable in fields far away from the electricity grid.

04
📵

Offline Local Control

Introducing fallback options like Bluetooth or physical pushbuttons for control during internet outages, enhancing system resilience. There is currently no alternative plan in case the internet connection fails — the local LAN WebServer provides partial offline diagnostics but not full control.

05
📊

Mobile App Enhancement

Upgrading the mobile interface with graphical dashboards, voice command support, multi-zone control, and scheduled spraying features. Currently there is an on/off toggle for turning the machine on and off; however, more features such as scheduling and logging need to be implemented. UI enhancement was not a focus of v1.

06
🔗

Multi-Inlet Plumbing

Redesigning for multiple pipe inlets with individual solenoid valves per zone. Currently, the entire system uses one unified inlet pipe. Multiple inlets with individual solenoids would significantly increase the practical value of the system for larger farm setups with multiple liquid types.
